### What does “doing something for one side to harm the other side” mean?

This phrase usually refers to a person who, out of interest, position, or commission, secretly or openly helps one side to strike, sabotage, or weaken the interests of the other side.

Depending on context, there are several corresponding Chinese idioms, colloquialisms, and legal / business terms for this kind of behavior:

**1. Common idioms and colloquialisms**

- **助纣为虐 (Help a tyrant do evil):** Helping a bad person do bad things, harming justice or the other side’s interests.
- **为虎作伥 (Act as a tiger’s accomplice):** Serving as a villain’s helper, helping him bully and harm others.
- **吃里扒外 (Eat from the inside, help the outside):** Taking benefits from one side, while secretly helping the other side to harm your own side.
- **两面三刀 / 阳奉阴违 (Two-faced / Outwardly compliant, inwardly defiant):** Appearing to obey one side on the surface, while behind its back doing things that harm it.
- **借刀杀人 / 暗箭伤人 (Kill with a borrowed knife / Wound with a hidden arrow):** Acting on someone’s instructions, or secretly striking for one side to frame or attack the other side.

**2. Workplace, business and legal terms**

- **利益冲突 (Conflict of Interest):** A person in a dual role acts for the interest of one side while harming the rights and interests the other side should have.
- **违背信义义务 (Breach of Fiduciary Duty):** In company law or trust law, a fiduciary — such as an executive, lawyer, or agent — acts for his own benefit or for a third party, to the detriment of the principal’s interests.
- **商业间谍 / 职务侵占 (Commercial espionage / Embezzlement in office):** Being employed by a competitor, or using one’s position to leak confidential information outward, thereby harming the original company.
- **恶意串通 (Malicious collusion):** In civil law, two parties conspiring together — or one party operating on behalf of the other — deliberately to harm the lawful rights and interests of the state, a collective, or a third party.

#### 1. Historical Review: From the “Century of Humiliation” to Sovereign Independence

When Sachs analyzed development paths for an African audience, he first drew a parallel between China’s and Africa’s historical experiences, pointing out that both places had deeply suffered Western imperialist aggression:

- **Invasion by great powers and war:** In the early 19th century, China was a huge economy with hundreds of millions of people, unified for two thousand years, but in 1839 Britain launched a war to dump opium, opening China’s “Century of Humiliation.” This was followed by two Opium Wars, the Taiping Rebellion with massive casualties, the First Sino-Japanese War in 1894, and Japan’s full-scale invasion of China in the 1930s-40s, which brought enormous loss of life.
- **Founding of the state and turning point:** It was not until the founding of New China in 1949 that China regained independence and sovereignty. After three decades of exploration and twists and turns, Reform and Opening in 1978 became the key watershed that changed China’s fate.

#### 2. Economic Miracle: The “Rule of 70” and Eliminating Extreme Poverty

Sachs used economic laws to explain to his audience how China went from destitution to prosperity:

- **∼10% compound annual growth:** From 1980 to 2020, over 40 years, China maintained an average annual economic growth rate of about 10%. According to the “Rule of 70” in economics — 70 divided by the growth rate equals the number of years needed to double — China’s economy doubled every 7 years, expanding total GDP by about 40 times over 40 years.
- **Historic poverty alleviation:** China reduced its extreme poverty rate from 80% to complete elimination by 2020. Sachs also shared his own field trip in 2018-2019 to the poorest county in Ningxia, confirming the significant improvement in rural infrastructure and basic living guarantees in China.

#### 3. The China-US Perception Gap: Why Did the US “Misread” China?

In his speech, Sachs humorously reviewed the evolution and misjudgment of US attitudes toward China:

- **From Cold War pawn to “world factory”:** In the 1970s, the US eased relations with China to counterbalance the Soviet Union, at that time seeing China only as a backward agricultural country. In the 1990s, after the Soviet Union collapsed, the US fell into blind confidence of being “forever No. 1,” seeing China only as a low-cost manufacturing base providing cheap clothing, toys, and auto parts, and even enjoying a low-inflation dividend as a result.
- **The shock of industrial upgrading:** It was not until around 2010 that the US was startled to realize how huge China’s economy had become. In 2015, China launched *Made in China 2025*, laying out ten key technology fields with foresight. Sachs pointed out that ten years later, a US Congressional report discovered in alarm that China had already ranked among the world leaders in seven of those fields — for example, the new energy vehicle industry went from zero to leading the world — and by then it was too late for the US to try to “contain” China through sanctions or blockades.

#### 4. Implications for Africa and Developing Countries

The original intention of Sachs’ speech was to provide development lessons for South Africa and the entire African continent. He concluded that China’s success relied on two stages: first using latecomer advantages to achieve “catching-up growth,” then shifting to “innovation-driven growth” through far-sighted industrial policy. He called on African countries to also formulate long-term plans similar to an “Africa Manufacturing 2035/2040,” and to follow a path of peaceful development and independent innovation.
