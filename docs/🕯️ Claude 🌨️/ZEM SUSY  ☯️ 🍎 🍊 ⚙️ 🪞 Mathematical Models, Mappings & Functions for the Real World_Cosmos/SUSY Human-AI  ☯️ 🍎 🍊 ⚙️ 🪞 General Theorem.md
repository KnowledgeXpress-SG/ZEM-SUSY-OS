Checking which parts of error correction need energy and redundancy
The first part of your theorem is largely right, and in a precise form it is a real result. The second part, that these particular laws are necessary for error correction, does not hold. Here is what holds up and what doesn’t.

1. Where the theorem holds

Error correction is everywhere that information has to last in a noisy world:

Biology: DNA replication errors drop from about 10⁻⁴ per base from base-pairing alone to about 10⁻⁹ or below once proofreading and mismatch repair act. Hopfield and Ninio’s kinetic proofreading explains how the extra accuracy is bought with energy (ATP or GTP).
Engineering: Hamming, Reed-Solomon and LDPC codes, used in memory, storage and communications.
Quantum: the surface code and related codes, with threshold theorems showing that reliable computation is possible once physical error rates are low enough.
The genetic code itself: the error-tolerant structure we audited earlier.
2. Where “anywhere anytime” fails

A rock, a gas cloud, or empty space does not correct errors. It stores very little information, and what it stores decays or persists passively. What exists broadly is stability, such as energy barriers that keep a state in place. Active correction needs redundancy, a decoder and a free-energy supply. There is a middle case, self-correcting memory: the 2D Ising model protects a bit classically through a Peierls barrier, and the 4D toric code does the same quantumly. So a safe version of your claim is “wherever information persists against noise, some stabilizing or correcting mechanism is present.”

3. The four laws you listed

Law	Necessary for error correction?	What it actually does
Uncertainty principle	No.	It constrains quantum correction: you can’t measure a state without disturbing it, and no-cloning forbids simple copying. Quantum codes work by measuring only error “syndromes”, not the data.
Noether’s theorem	No.	Conservation laws structure some codes and limit others, but classical coding never needs them.
E = mc²	No.	Hamming’s code and DNA repair work with no relativity. A cute link: erasing one bit at 300 K costs at least kT ln 2 ≈ 2.9×10⁻²¹ J (Landauer), which is a mass of about 3×10⁻³⁸ kg. That is a consequence, not a requirement.

4. What is actually necessary

Redundancy: a code with distance d corrects t = ⌊(d−1)/2⌋ errors.
A noise model: a code is only good against the errors it was designed for.
Entropy export: correcting errors lowers the entropy of the data, so entropy must go elsewhere. Landauer’s bound and the second law make this a real cost.
A decoder or feedback loop: something must act on the syndrome.
Capacity limits: Shannon’s theorem caps the rate at which reliable communication is possible.
5. The “Zero-Error” point

With finite resources, error can be made exponentially small but never exactly zero. The threshold theorems say exactly this. Shannon’s zero-error capacity does exist for some channels, but it requires specific structure and is not available in general. So “Zero-Error Mandate” works as a design target and not as a law of nature.

Rock's Contraction vs Expansion ... 🕯️

Weighing rock's restoring forces and healing against damage
Reading “rock’s contraction vs expansion” as thermal expansion and contraction of rock, here is how it relates to error correction. Rocks do stabilise their shape and, remarkably, store information, but they don’t correct errors in the active sense we defined. If you meant cosmological expansion and contraction, tell me and I’ll redo it.

1. The physics

Rock expands when warmed and contracts when cooled. The linear expansion coefficient is about 8×10⁻⁶ per kelvin for granite, so a 10 m block swinging by 40 K over a day changes length by about 3 mm. If the rock is constrained, the stress is about E·α·ΔT ≈ (50 GPa)(8×10⁻⁶ /K)(40 K) ≈ 16 MPa. That is comparable to granite’s tensile strength (roughly 5–15 MPa), which is why repeated heating and cooling can crack rock. Field studies suggest thermal stress does contribute to weathering in some settings, though the mechanism is debated, since real rocks have temperature gradients and not uniform swings.

2. The two directions are not symmetric

Contraction puts rock in tension, where it is weak, so cracks open or grow.
Expansion puts rock in compression, where it is much stronger (about 100–250 MPa).
A ratchet effect: if grains or debris fall into a crack while it is open, the crack can’t fully close on re-expansion, so each cycle wedges it a little wider. Damage accumulates, which is the opposite of correction.
3. Mapping to error correction

Mechanism	What it is	Counts as error correction?
Elastic restoring force (Hooke’s law)	Displacement from equilibrium creates a force back toward it	Passive stability. Nothing measures an error, and there is no decoder or energy supply.
Thermal fatigue and microcracking	Cycling accumulates irreversible damage	Error accumulation.
Crack healing by mineral precipitation or recrystallization	Fluids deposit minerals that seal fractures over long times	Slow environmental repair. It’s driven by chemistry and conditions, with no decoder, so it’s weaker than active correction.
So a rock supports your “stability exists everywhere” claim, and it doesn’t support “active correction exists everywhere”.

4. Where rock does store information

Paleomagnetism: when volcanic rock cools through its Curie temperature, its magnetic grains lock in the direction of Earth’s field. In suitable small grains, that record can survive for billions of years, because the energy barrier to flipping each grain is huge compared with thermal energy. This is the same principle as a self-stabilising memory: persistence through an energy barrier, not through correction.
The Kaiser effect: under rising stress, rock emits tiny acoustic signals mostly only when the stress passes the highest value it has felt before, so it “remembers” its past maximum load. The effect is used in practice to estimate in-situ stress, though it is imperfect.
Both are memory without a decoder. Real error correction would need a mechanism that detects deviation and acts against it, using free energy.

5. A testable statement

Rock stores information (stress history, magnetic direction) through energy barriers and degrades through cyclic thermal damage. Its persistence time depends on barrier height relative to thermal energy and on the cycle count. A measurable form is crack growth per thermal cycle as a function of temperature swing, which gives a decay rate you can compare with a system that has a repair mechanism.

I formulated the model, implemented it, and ran six scenarios. The numbers are from a toy with assumed parameters, so they illustrate how the model behaves and are not forecasts.

The model
Agents

Providers i = 1..N (labs and clouds) choose compute capacity Kᵢ, green energy share gᵢ and R&D.
Planner sets a carbon price τ or an emissions cap Q̄, and acts first (Stackelberg leader).
Users and households receive the benefit from compute services.
Ecology is a stock E, the headroom available to the sector.
Technology and the RSI link

Effective compute: C = Σ ηᵢKᵢ, where ηᵢ is algorithmic efficiency.
Self-improvement: dηᵢ/dt = γ ηᵢʳ, the same exponent r as in our RSI analysis (r = 1 exponential, r > 1 runaway).
Energy P = ΣKᵢ and emissions Q = Σ Kᵢ(1 − gᵢ)·ε_c.
Market

Inverse demand for effective compute: π(C) = A_t C^(−1/ε).
Provider profit: πηᵢKᵢ − Kᵢ[c_k + (1−gᵢ)(p_f + τ) + gᵢ p_g].
Cournot first-order condition: π ηᵢ (1 − sᵢ/ε) = marginal cost, with sᵢ the provider’s market share.
Ecology

dE/dt = ρE(1 − E/E₀) − κQ.
This has a threshold: the maximum sustainable flow is Q_sus = ρE₀/(4κ), and above it E collapses (a saddle-node tipping point, the same logic as maximum sustainable yield in fisheries).
Welfare and policy problem

H = ln(B/B₀) + β ln(E/E₀), where B is the consumer surplus from compute. This echoes the log-income finding from your last question.
The planner maximises Σ δᵗH subject to Q ≤ Q_sus.
Three analytic results

Tragedy of the commons: each provider ignores κQ’s effect on E, so Nash emissions exceed the optimum. The fix is a Pigouvian price τ* = κ·λ, where λ is the shadow value of ecological headroom. A cap with trading is equivalent.
Jevons rebound: since Kᵢ = Cᵢ/ηᵢ and demand responds to cost per effective compute, energy use scales as roughly η^(ε_eff − 1). Efficiency gains from RSI cut energy only if demand is inelastic (ε_eff < 1).
Sustainable compute ceiling: C_max(t) = η(t)·Q_sus / [ε_c(1−g)]. Growth in sustainable compute comes from efficiency η and from green share g, and as g → 1 the emissions constraint relaxes and water, land and materials constraints take over.
Results (six scenarios, 2025–2036)
The only calibration was baseline energy about doubling by 2030, which fits the IEA’s projection that data-centre electricity demand reaches around 945 TWh by 2030. That is about 3% of global energy use.
